indepth:
  - heading: "Why computer science leans on discrete maths, not calculus"
    body: >
      "Discrete" just means countable and separate, as opposed to
      continuous — and it turns out most of computer science is discrete by
      nature. A computer stores information in distinct bits, runs a
      distinct sequence of instructions, and works with data structures
      made of distinct, individually addressable items. That's why this
      module leans on sets, logic, and combinatorics rather than the
      calculus you might associate with "advanced maths" — the maths that
      actually mirrors how a computer works isn't about smooth continuous
      change, it's about countable, distinct things and the rules for
      combining them.
  - heading: "Sets: the basic unit of 'a collection of things'"
    body: >
      A set is simply a well-defined collection of distinct objects, called
      its elements — a set of numbers, a set of usernames, a set of valid
      inputs to a function. What makes sets useful in computer science is
      the small toolkit of operations you can do with them: union
      (everything in either set), intersection (only what's in both), and
      difference (what's in one but not the other). These aren't just
      abstract exercises — they're the same operations underneath a
      database query that filters or combines records, and the same
      thinking behind classifying data into non-overlapping categories.
  - heading: "Logic: making 'true' and 'false' precise"
    body: >
      Logic gives you a precise way to combine statements that are either
      true or false, using connectives like AND, OR, and NOT, so you can
      reason about complex conditions without ambiguity. This is the exact
      same logic that shows up as a programming language's if-statements
      and boolean conditions — "if the user is logged in AND has admin
      rights" is a logical proposition before it's ever a line of code.
      Learning to manipulate these statements formally, rather than just
      intuitively, is what lets you spot when a condition is
      accidentally always true, always false, or doesn't actually mean what
      you think it means.
  - heading: "Where the numerical and statistical strands fit in"
    body: >
      Alongside sets and logic, this module introduces numerical methods
      (techniques for solving problems computationally when there's no neat
      formula) and a first pass at probability and statistics — how likely
      something is, and how to summarise a set of data with numbers like a
      mean or a standard deviation rather than staring at every individual
      value. Both come back constantly once you're actually programming:
      numerical methods when an algorithm needs to approximate an answer
      rather than compute it exactly, and probability/statistics whenever a
      system needs to make a decision based on uncertain or noisy data.
