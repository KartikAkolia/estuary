keyTerms:
  - term: "Software development lifecycle (SDLC)"
    definition: "The structured sequence of phases — requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, maintenance — a software product moves through."
  - term: "Waterfall model"
    definition: "A linear, plan-based process model where each lifecycle phase is completed in sequence before the next begins."
  - term: "Agile"
    definition: "A family of iterative, incremental process models (e.g. Scrum, Kanban) favouring short cycles and working software over following a rigid upfront plan."
  - term: "Requirements specification"
    definition: "A document capturing what a software system must do, agreed with stakeholders, and used as the basis for design and testing."
  - term: "Version control"
    definition: "A system (e.g. Git) for tracking and coordinating changes to source code over time and across contributors."

indepth:
  - heading: "The lifecycle: the same six questions, every project"
    body: >
      Any piece of software, however it gets built, ends up moving through
      the same rough set of questions: what does it need to do
      (requirements), how will it be structured (design), how does it get
      built (implementation), does it actually work (testing), how does it
      reach real users (deployment), and what happens once it's out there
      and things need fixing or changing (maintenance). What differs
      between projects isn't whether these phases happen — it's whether
      they happen once, in strict order, or repeatedly, in small
      overlapping cycles. That difference is essentially what separates
      the two major process models this module compares.
  - heading: "Waterfall: finish each phase before the next starts"
    body: >
      The waterfall model runs the lifecycle once, top to bottom, like
      water flowing downhill — requirements are gathered and locked in
      early, then design, then implementation, then testing, with each
      stage expected to be complete before the next begins. Its strength is
      predictability: heavy upfront documentation, a clear plan, and a
      structure that suits projects where the requirements are genuinely
      well understood in advance and unlikely to change much. Its weakness
      is the same trait — because going back a phase is expensive, a wrong
      assumption caught late (say, during testing) can be costly to fix,
      since it means unwinding decisions made much earlier.
  - heading: "Agile: repeat a small version of the lifecycle often"
    body: >
      Agile approaches (Scrum being the most common one you'll meet) grew
      out of frustration with exactly that waterfall weakness. Instead of
      one long pass through the lifecycle, agile teams repeat a
      compressed version of it every sprint — typically two to four weeks —
      shipping a small working increment each time and adjusting based on
      real feedback rather than an upfront plan. This trades some of
      waterfall's predictability for the ability to change direction
      without having wasted months building the wrong thing. In practice,
      most real teams land somewhere between the two — planning at a high
      level up front, then executing iteratively.
  - heading: "Why this module exists before you've built anything big"
    body: >
      None of this is really about picking a "correct" methodology — it's
      about recognising that how a team organises its work has a direct
      effect on whether a project succeeds, independent of how good any
      individual line of code is. Requirements gathering, version control,
      and choosing a process model appropriate to a project's actual risk
      and uncertainty are the practical skills this module is building,
      ahead of the group and individual coursework later in the degree
      where they'll actually get used under real constraints.
